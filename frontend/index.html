Method notes

States come from a rule engine and a trained model, fused, smoothed over a seven-sample majority vote, and held by a state machine with hysteresis. Deltas are measured against the personal baseline recorded at the start of this session. Early warnings are short-horizon linear projections of the current trend.

Transparent decision guide

How Saarthi works

What actually runs, in order — sensor to session report.

  1. 📡SenseHR + GSR, ~1/s
  2. ⚖Calibrateyour own baseline
  3. ∿Cleansmooth + 30 s window
  4. 📊Deciderules + ML
  5. 🔒Stabilisevote + hold times
  6. 📋Act & logbreathe, report
  1. 1

    Sensor input

    Hardware

    A pulse sensor and two GSR electrodes on an Arduino send one text line per second over an HC-05 Bluetooth link, like GSR:512,HR:74.

    Why
    Heart rate and skin conductance both rise with autonomic arousal, and two signals are harder to fool than one.
    Key decision
    Any line that does not match the expected pattern (for example “Place finger”) is dropped rather than guessed at.
    Output
    hr gsr once per second
  2. 2

    Personal calibration

    ~30 s

    Before any state is shown, Saarthi watches a rolling ~30 second window and locks it as your resting reference only once it is genuinely steady.

    Why
    A resting heart rate of 58 and one of 88 are both normal. Every later number is a change from your own baseline, never a population cutoff.
    Key decision
    Lock only if the window is calm: HR spread ≤ 15 bpm and GSR spread ≤ 50 units. If not, the window keeps sliding until a steady stretch appears.
    Output
    baseline_hr baseline_gsr — no state is displayed until this exists
  3. 3

    Signal processing

    30 s window

    Each reading is range-checked, averaged over the last 3 samples, and pushed into a rolling 30 second buffer.

    Why
    Raw wearable data is spiky. A single bad sample should never move the state on screen.
    Key decision
    Physiologically implausible values are discarded outright — HR outside 40–210 bpm, GSR outside 0–1200 units.
    Output
    smoothed hr/gsr + a 30 s window of history
  4. 4

    Feature extraction

    10 numbers

    That window is reduced to the same ten numbers every time — the only thing either decision layer ever sees.

    mean HRHR spreadHR trend mean GSRGSR spreadGSR trend ΔHR vs baselineΔGSR vs baseline stress indexconfidence
    Why
    One fixed vector means the rules, the model and the forecast all reason over identical evidence.
    Key decision
    Stress index = ΔHR + 0.05 × ΔGSR, which puts both signals on one comparable scale. Confidence (0–1) scores how full and clean the window is.
    Output
    feature vector[10]
  5. 5

    Rule-based detection

    Tier 1 · decides

    Transparent thresholds on the change from your baseline produce the first verdict.

    StressΔHR > 6 or ΔGSR > 40
    AnxietyΔHR > 12 and ΔGSR > 80
    Settlingelevated, but HR now clearly falling → step down one level
    Why
    Every decision stays explainable: you can always point at the two numbers that caused it.
    Key decision
    Anxiety requires both channels. If confidence drops below 0.5, the verdict is demoted one level rather than raising a shaky alarm.
    Output
    rule_state: CALM / STRESS / ANXIETY
  6. 6

    ML model

    Tier 2 · second opinion

    A Random Forest (100 trees, depth 5) reads the same ten numbers and returns one binary call with a probability.

    Why
    It can pick up combinations of trend and variability that fixed thresholds miss.
    Key decision
    It is deliberately binary — CALM or ANXIETY only. It has no notion of STRESS or RECOVERY, and it is fed raw, unscaled features exactly as it was trained.
    Output
    ml_state + probability
  7. 7

    Rule + ML fusion

    Confirm-only gate

    The model confirms the rules. It does not outrank them.

    Rules: ANXIETY+ML: ANXIETY ≥ 0.75→ANXIETY corroborated
    Rules: STRESS+ML: ANXIETY→STRESS not escalated
    Why
    Measured on held-out data, the model reports ANXIETY for mild elevation with almost the same confidence as for full arousal — it separates elevated from calm well, but carries no severity information.
    Key decision
    The gate is probability × confidence ≥ 0.75, and it can only agree with an existing ANXIETY verdict. It can never turn moderate stress into anxiety.
    Output
    fused_state + its source: rules or both
  8. 8

    Smoothing & state machine

    Anti-flicker

    Two guards sit between the verdict and the screen: a majority vote over the last 7 readings, then a state machine that enforces how long a change must persist.

    Calm→3 s Stress→3 s Anxiety→5 s Recovery→5 s Calm
    Why
    A display that flickers between states every second is unreadable and untrustworthy in front of an audience.
    Key decision
    Anxiety can never jump straight back to calm — it must pass through recovery. Calm cannot jump straight to anxiety either, unless the stress index is extreme.
    Output
    final state — the one big word on the dashboard
  9. 9

    Predictive early warning

    ≤ 30 s horizon

    While the stress index is rising, its slope is projected forward in a straight line to the next rule threshold, producing a countdown.

    Why
    Reaching someone before a peak is more useful than announcing it afterwards.
    Key decision
    Shown only after calibration, only while genuinely rising, only when confidence ≥ 0.5, and never further than 30 s ahead. Two falling readings clear it immediately.
    Output
    predicted state + seconds — and the report later records how many forecasts actually arrived

    This is a straight-line extrapolation of the last few seconds, not a learned forecasting model.

  10. 10

    Guided breathing & recovery

    4·4·4·4

    The dashboard offers a box-breathing exercise — inhale, hold, exhale, hold, four seconds each — and then measures what happened next.

    Why
    Detection on its own is a verdict. The exercise turns it into something the wearer can act on immediately.
    Key decision
    Start and stop are recorded by the backend, not the browser, so a refresh mid-exercise loses nothing. A 60 s recovery window afterwards captures the HR change.
    Output
    intervention record + measured HR change, shown on the recovery card and in the report
  11. 11

    Session lifecycle & report

    Idle → Report

    A session moves Idle → Calibrating → Monitoring → Ended, checkpointing every 10 seconds, and ends with a report you can open or download.

    Why
    A live number is a moment. A session is the evidence trail: what happened, when, and how much of it to trust.
    Key decision
    Every report is graded for reliability first — Insufficient under 60 s of monitoring, Limited under 3 minutes, otherwise Good. A weak session has its metrics withheld and says why, rather than presenting thin numbers as solid ones.
    Output
    report as on-screen summary, HTML or CSV — plus episodes, forecasts issued vs confirmed, and interventions

Stated plainly

Limitations & future work

These are real properties of the build in front of you, not hypotheticals.

  • Response latency of up to ~30 seconds. Every decision reads a 30 second trailing window, so a change has to occupy a meaningful part of that window before the state moves. Hold times add a few more seconds on purpose. Saarthi reports a sustained shift, not an instant one.
  • No accelerometer — movement is not separated from arousal. There is no motion sensor in this build. Climbing stairs and a panic response can look similar on HR, and the system cannot tell them apart. Readings assume a seated, reasonably still wearer.
  • The ML model is binary. It only distinguishes CALM from ANXIETY. Everything nuanced on screen — stress, recovery, the ordering between them — comes from the rule engine and the state machine, not the model.
  • The ML accuracy figure is optimistic, and we know why. The reported 95.7% comes from a random split of rows drawn from a 30 second sliding window, so neighbouring rows overlap almost completely and near-duplicates land on both sides of the split. Re-tested on grouped, non-overlapping segments the same model scores roughly 73%, and about 45% on an unseen episode. Treat 95.7% as an upper bound, not a performance claim.
  • Calibration inherits your condition at that moment. The baseline is whatever steady window it locks onto. Calibrate straight after running up the stairs and the reference sits too high, which makes every later change look smaller than it is. Start a session at rest.
  • GSR is in raw sensor units. The 40 and 80 cutoffs are internally consistent but are not microsiemens, so they cannot be compared directly against published EDA figures.
  • One small dataset, no external validation. The model was trained on this project’s own bench data, and labels were generated by the same baseline logic the rules use. A high score largely shows the model reproduces that internal logic consistently.

Proposed Future work — none of the following is implemented in the system being demonstrated.

  • Add an accelerometer so motion can be subtracted before a rise is read as arousal.
  • Validate on held-out people, not held-out rows — a multi-subject dataset with subject-independent splits, which the current data cannot support.
  • A multi-class model that can recognise STRESS and RECOVERY instead of leaving those to the rules alone.
  • HRV features (inter-beat intervals) alongside raw heart rate, which the literature treats as a stronger arousal marker.
  • Calibrated GSR in microsiemens, so thresholds become comparable with published research.
  • A longer, research-grade baseline option for settings where a 5–10 minute rest is practical.
Important: Saarthi is an educational wellness prototype. It does not diagnose, treat, or monitor any medical or mental-health condition, and its output is not a clinical measurement. If you are concerned about symptoms, consult a qualified healthcare professional.

How the dashboard estimates a state

Baseline-relative

A quick view of how the dashboard turns live readings into a reliable wellness estimate.

Evidence-informed

Personalized multi-signal monitoring

HR and EDA/GSR both index sympathetic (autonomic) arousal, and clinical guidance calls for comparing each person against their own baseline rather than a fixed population cutoff. [1]

Bolpagni et al. 2024, personalized-wearable stress scoping review ↗
Known limitation

No consumer wearable is a validated stress diagnostic

A 2023 review of wearable health research concludes consumer devices "are not yet at the point of being able to reliably detect when a person is having a stress response" — this project inherits that limitation. [2]

Roos & Slavich 2023, wearable-limitations review ↗

Where the specific numbers (6/12 bpm, 40/80 GSR units, ~30s baseline) come from

The literature robustly supports the direction of these signals — HR and GSR rise with sympathetic arousal — but it does not hand over one universal cutoff. Reported HR reactivity to acute stress ranges from roughly −7 to +36 bpm across individuals in controlled studies [7][8], so this project's 6 bpm (stress) and 12 bpm (anxiety) cutoffs sit inside that published range as a responsiveness/false-alarm tradeoff — they are engineering heuristics tuned on this project's own bench data, not values copied from a specific clinical protocol.

The GSR figures are a separate caveat: the HC-05 sensor reports a raw, uncalibrated 0–1200 sensor unit (see bt_reader.py/pipeline.py), not microsiemens (μS). Published EDA thresholds are stated in μS, so the "40" and "80" cutoffs here are internally consistent but are not directly comparable to any microsiemens figure in the literature.

The baseline window is a similar tradeoff: published stress-baseline protocols typically rest a subject for 5–10 minutes before treating a reading as a stable reference [9]. This project calibrates in ~30 seconds for usability, which means the baseline is noisier than a research-grade one — a deliberate UX tradeoff, disclosed here rather than hidden.

Tier 2 model

How the ML model is used

Random Forest
Model inputs
10 engineered features: HR/GSR averages, variation, trends, changes from baseline, stress index, and signal quality.
Model output
Binary CALM or ANXIETY. It does not independently classify STRESS or RECOVERY.
Training design
100-tree, depth-5 Random Forest trained with an 80/20 development split using the project's labelled dataset.
Safety gate
ML can override only for ANXIETY when model probability × signal quality ≥ 0.75; otherwise the rule model remains in control.
Measured accuracy
95.7% on a held-out 20% test split (1,067 samples; 96.7% on the 4,268-sample training split). Class balance: 3,441 CALM / 1,894 ANXIETY across 5,335 rows.
Literature benchmark
Published Random Forest models on wearable HR/HRV + GSR stress data report accuracy from roughly 80% up to >99% depending on dataset and validation protocol [5][6] — this project's 95.7% is plausible within that spread, but the studies differ in sensors, subjects, and label source, so it is a sanity check, not a like-for-like comparison.

Validation status: this local development model has no external clinical validation. Its output is a supporting signal, not medical evidence or a diagnosis.

Read the accuracy number carefully: the training labels were assigned using the same delta-from-baseline logic the rule engine uses, on the same engineered features. A high test score mostly shows the model reproduces that internal logic consistently — it is not evidence the labels themselves correspond to clinically confirmed anxiety, and it does not measure real-world performance on a different person, sensor, or day.

Early-warning layer

How the trend forecast is computed

Linear extrapolation
What it uses
The current window's HR trend and GSR trend (least-squares slopes), converted to a rate of change of the same stress index the rule engine already uses.
How the countdown is made
It measures the gap between the current stress index and the next state's rule threshold, then divides by the current rate of rise — a straight-line projection, not a machine-learned forecast.
When it is shown
Only once calibration is complete, only while HR and/or GSR are genuinely rising, only when signal confidence is ≥ 0.5, and capped at a 30-second horizon.
When it is cleared
Immediately on two consecutive falling-HR readings (trend reversal), once the state machine reaches the forecast state, or if the horizon would exceed 30s.

Limitation: this is a same-signal extrapolation of the last few seconds, not a validated predictive model — it assumes the current rate of change continues linearly, which short bursts of movement or noise can break.

📋 How to use the device ▾
1
Wear the device properly

Strap the sensor securely to your fingertip or wrist as instructed. Ensure the optical HR sensor and GSR electrodes have firm, consistent skin contact before powering on.

2
Power on — listen for 2 beeps

When the device boots it plays 2 short buzzer beeps. This confirms firmware has initialised and Bluetooth is broadcasting. Wait for the status bar above to turn green before starting a session.

3
One buzz = one data point recorded

After connecting, the buzzer pulses once per second. Each buzz means HR and GSR values have been sampled and sent to this dashboard. The live chart updates in sync.

⚠
Keep your hand still — no accelerometer on board

This device does not include an accelerometer, so motion artefacts cannot be filtered automatically. Any movement of the hand or wrist while wearing the device will cause false HR spikes that the ML model will misclassify as stress or anxiety.

✅ Correct use: wear the device, place both hands on a flat surface, and sit calmly for the duration of the session.

4
Calibration window (~30 s)

The first 30 seconds are used to establish your personal baseline HR and GSR. Sit still during this period. The footer will change from "Baseline calibrating…" to show your baseline values once complete.
